## Thought experiment one: the networks

Build a large set of artificial neural networks. Give every network the same architecture, initial parameters, learning algorithm, and computational budget. Divide them randomly between two training environments.

The first environment contains varied, well-labelled examples presented in a useful order. The second contains fewer examples, noisier labels, and less helpful feedback. After training, give every network the same unfamiliar test. Record not only its final score, but how quickly it adapts to new examples and whether what it learns transfers to a different task.

We should expect the groups to perform differently. Since their starting designs were held constant, the experiment would attribute that difference to training. The better-trained networks may now recognise more patterns, learn faster, and generalise further. Those are real properties of the finished networks, even though they were not fixed properties of the networks at initialization.

Now discard the training records and hand the finished networks to a second researcher. This researcher sees only their test results. The results reveal differences in present performance. But can they reveal which networks began with greater capacity? In this version of the experiment, none did. Can the researcher infer how each would have performed under the other training environment? Not from the test alone. That would require resetting and retraining them.

We can vary the experiment. Give the networks different architectures as well as different training histories. Their scores now reflect both. A sufficiently elaborate study might estimate the average contribution of each factor, because networks can be copied, reset, and rerun. A score from one finished network still does not perform that separation by itself.

## Thought experiment two: the children

Begin instead with a large group of newborn children. Imagine that we can measure their genetics, prenatal conditions, health, and early neurophysiology in extraordinary detail. We then assign them randomly to developmental environments which differ in nutrition, security, language, schooling, play, stress, and opportunities to solve problems.

At several ages, give them unfamiliar cognitive tasks. Some test memory, some abstraction, some spatial reasoning, and some the speed of learning a new rule. Change the testing conditions too. Measure each child when rested and tired, relaxed and anxious, practised and unpractised. Later, exchange some of the environments and see whether the children's learning rates or rankings change.

Such an experiment could distinguish several possibilities. Stable differences appearing early and persisting across environments would support a strong role for initial differences. Large group differences created by the assigned environments would support a strong developmental role. Changes after the environments were exchanged would show continued malleability. Different patterns across tasks would count against intelligence being wholly unitary. The likely result could contain some of each.

We cannot actually run this experiment. More importantly, we cannot run its cleanest comparison: reset one child to birth and let the same child develop again under different conditions. Each person reaches an IQ test through one history, and that history has already altered the person being measured.

An IQ score may still be stable, useful, and predictive. The question raised by the two experiments is narrower. When a test examines a developed system once, how much can its result tell us about present performance, and how much can it tell us about the unobserved capacity with which that system began?
