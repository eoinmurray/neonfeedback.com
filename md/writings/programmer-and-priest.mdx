## scene 1 decisions

1. **Opening passage.** “You know, we don’t usually have cappuccino after eleven here,” he said as I ordered mine.

    He had been explaining this to me for twenty years. I had continued to disappoint him.

    He took out a cigarette.

    “At least a cappuccino won’t kill me.”

1. **Old friends.** The narrator and the other priest are old friends, meeting again at the symposium. Their coffee correction, national teasing, comfortable silences, and willingness to walk together draw on established affection. The narrator seeks sympathy from someone whose judgment already matters to him. The other priest's eventual challenge comes from within that friendship, not from a stranger assuming moral authority. How they first met remains unspecified. The character draws on an old Italian friend of the author's for emotional texture and comic rhythm, while his priesthood, biography, and role remain fictional; do not assume that the real friend shares the character's institutional loyalties or beliefs.

1. **The symposium.** The narrator is in Rome for a fictional Church symposium on “Artificial Intelligence and Human Dignity.” He attends as a parish practitioner concerned with pastoral life, not as an AI expert. The institutional discussion of humanity contrasts quietly with his difficulty understanding one particular human. The event remains mostly offstage.

1. **The afternoon break.** After a panel on listening, the narrator and another priest go across the street for coffee. A possible observation: “The morning had been devoted to whether a machine could listen. After lunch, three speakers had demonstrated that a person need not.” The narrator orders a cappuccino. During the break, he remarks to his companion, “There's an evangelical pastor in America who's made an AI version of himself.” Use “pastor,” rather than “priest,” for the American minister. This brings the symposium's subject into ordinary conversation and quietly anticipates the story's questions about listening, reassurance, and the desire to remain available and necessary. A factual model is Ron Carpenter, whose ministry promotes an AI app based on his sermon archive; the dialogue need not name him. See [Ron Carpenter Ministries](https://roncarpenter.com/) and [the app's description](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/ron-carpenter-ai/id6737125441).

1. **The coffee correction and cigarette.** “You know, here we don't usually have those after breakfast,” the other priest says. “I've been indoors since breakfast.” The other priest accepts this without appearing convinced and takes out his cigarettes. He smokes outside while the narrator waits for his coffee. The narrator does not smoke. The companion is called “the other priest,” not introduced by nationality; local familiarity and conversation establish his background without an exaggerated accent or mannerisms.

1. **From meetings to calls.** The programmer and narrator first became friends through local, face-to-face conversations. While the narrator is away in Rome, they have continued those conversations through scheduled calls. Establish this briefly so that the message follows naturally from the friendship described in the main story.

1. **The WhatsApp message.** The programmer writes: “Sorry, can't make our call today. Probably can't make the next one either.” He withdraws without formally declaring the friendship over. The message leaves his intentions uncertain; the narrator interprets it as rejection.

1. **The first exchange.** The other priest asks, “Bad news?” The narrator replies, “A friend cancelling.” “Today?” “And, apparently, the future.” The other priest offers him a cigarette; he declines.

1. **The bid for sympathy.** The narrator genuinely misses the programmer while resenting what he experiences as the withdrawal of his trust. He wants his companion to recognise his care and agree that he has been treated unfairly. He has travelled to the centre of the Church but remains preoccupied with one man's refusal of his attention.

1. **The reason for telling.** “We used to speak quite often,” the narrator says. “What about?” “That's difficult to explain.” The other priest looks at his cigarette and says, “Start.” The narrator begins with the wedding reception, seeking to establish that this was a real friendship and that his willingness to listen mattered.

1. **The walk.** After finishing his cigarette, the other priest invites the narrator to walk around St Peter's Square and the surrounding streets. The account continues as they walk; it does not fit into one cigarette. Crossings, shade, directions, and things they notice provide occasional interruptions. The narrator controls the story's route more carefully than the walk's: “Where are we going?” “Nowhere in particular.” “Good.” “You were telling me why he stopped calling.” “I was telling you how we met.” “Yes.”

1. **The battle picture and hypothetical war.** A battle painting prompts the narrator to ask who would win a war between their countries and confidently nominate his own. “We would win, obviously.” “Possibly. Half of us would fight on your side.” The other priest's self-mockery punctures the narrator's patriotic certainty; it is banter between these men, not an assertion about national courage. Keep the home country unnamed through “we,” “you,” and “your lot.” A possible visual is a reproduction of the Vatican's Battle of the Pons Milvius in a bookshop window; this placement is a fictional staging choice. The original is inside the Vatican Museums, not something they encounter on an ordinary outdoor walk.

1. **Football and expectations.** Talk of Spain's recent World Cup victory lets the narrator tease his companion about his team's failure to qualify. “You didn't qualify either.” “We didn't expect to.” The narrator salvages national pride by changing the standard of success. The reference fits a setting after July 2026; avoid specifying the year in dialogue unless needed. “We” and “you” preserve the unnamed home country and avoid introducing the other priest through a nationality label.

1. **Corporate power and papal access.** Returning briefly to the symposium, they discuss prominent technology figures being welcomed at the Vatican. The narrator thinks they are received like heads of state; the other priest wonders whether some companies now wield comparable power. This is their interpretation of corporate influence and ceremonial attention, not a claim that executives receive formal diplomatic status or that papal audiences are reserved for heads of state. Recent factual anchors include the papal audience for Cisco's CEO in 2024 and Anthropic cofounder Christopher Olah's participation in the May 2026 presentation of Magnifica humanitas. The fictional symposium need not be either historical event.

1. **The Church's diminished authority.** Both lament a perceived loss of the Church's influence while discussing the companies' rise. Their concern about unaccountable power is mixed with institutional wounded pride. The narrator can recognise concentrated power here while resisting similar arguments from the programmer. The other priest is capable of irreverence without being free of nostalgia or institutional loyalty.

1. **The Vatican in Sweden.** Looking towards the Vatican, the other priest says, “If we could move the Vatican to Sweden, we'd have invented flying cars by now.” He says it as a rueful lament, with the weariness of a familiar thought rather than the satisfaction of delivering a punchline. The absurdity remains funny, but he is mourning possibilities he believes his country has lost under the institution to which he still belongs. This is his feeling, not a historical explanation the story endorses. His regret about the Church's weight on Italian life coexists with his regret at its declining influence; recognising the contradiction does not free him from it.

1. **The frame's rhythm.** Spread the war, football, corporate-power, and flying-car exchanges across returns to the walking frame rather than placing a routine of jokes before the wedding reception. They establish affection and imperfect self-awareness. Allow surroundings to complicate a remark occasionally, without turning every building into a symbol. The other priest also wants shade, has opinions about the symposium, and knows a shorter route; he is a companion rather than a sequence of corrective pronouncements.

1. **The return to the frame.** After the account, the other priest brings up _St Alphonsus Liguori_ and his advice to restrict repetitive confession, connecting it to the programmer's repeated, short-lived relief. The narrator wanted sympathy for losing a friend and instead encounters a question about his own role. His colleague can challenge him without becoming an infallible moral authority. Scrupulosity is not named in the opening, preserving its gradual recognition through the story.

1. **The non-redemptive withdrawal.** The programmer is withdrawing from conversations with this priest, not ceasing to need an answer. The uncertain future of their contact does not resolve his compulsive cycle or political complicity, and telling the story does not give the narrator a moral awakening.

1. **Factual reference: football.** [FIFA's 2026 final report](https://www.fifa.com/en/tournaments/mens/worldcup/canadamexicousa2026/articles/spain-argentina-final-report-highlights) records Spain's victory; [FIFA's qualifying report](https://www.fifa.com/en/tournaments/mens/worldcup/canadamexicousa2026/articles/european-qualifiers-play-off-final-review) records Italy's elimination, and [the FAI's match report](https://www.fai.ie/latest/match-report-czechia-2-2-republic-ireland-4-3-pens-heartbreak-prague/) records Ireland's elimination. These support the setting; the narrator's low expectations are his joke, not a claim about every supporter.

1. **Factual reference: Vatican and art.** [Vatican News on Cisco's audience](https://www.vaticannews.va/en/vatican-city/news/2024-04/cisco-pontifical-academy-life-rome-call-ethics-pope-audience.html) and [Vatican News on the encyclical presentation](https://www.vaticannews.va/it/papa/news/2026-05/papa-leone-xiv-enciclica-magnifica-humanitas-olah-anthropic.html) support the corporate encounters. [The Vatican Museums catalogue](https://www.museivaticani.va/content/museivaticani/en/collezioni/musei/stanze-di-raffaello/sala-di-costantino/battaglia-di-costantino-contro-massenzio.html) locates the Battle of the Pons Milvius in the Room of Constantine. The café, symposium, walk, and placement of a reproduction are fictional.

## scene 2 decisions

1. **The wedding reception.** The programmer is a friend of the groom. The priest is an old family friend who officiated and stayed for the reception. The wedding has an Irish social setting without naming the country in the story. The priest knows the people involved but cannot know exactly how each person feels.

1. **The omitted brother.** During his thank-you speech, the groom forgets to acknowledge his brother Fergal, who has helped with the wedding. The programmer, tipsy rather than conspicuously drunk, tries to prompt him discreetly: “Fergal. You forgot Fergal.” It comes out louder than intended.

1. **The interruption.** The groom thanks Fergal and continues. A few people laugh; someone turns around. There is an actual interruption, but its significance remains ordinary and uncertain. The programmer's wish to include Fergal is sincere; the scene does not require either perfect innocence or a major offence.

1. **The coffee table.** Later, the priest and programmer meet at the serving table. The priest lightly laments the filter coffee: “I see they've kept something back for the penitential rite,” he says, lifting the pot. The programmer smiles. Shared humour opens the encounter before reassurance becomes its purpose.

1. **The question beneath the question.** The programmer asks whether the priest heard him during the speech. A possible narration: “Everyone had heard him, but I understood that wasn't quite his question.” The priest recognises the request for comfort without recognising a compulsive pattern, and is pleased to be able to help.

1. **Explanation and relief.** The programmer explains that he was trying to help Fergal. The priest offers proportionate reassurance. The programmer visibly relaxes, makes a good joke, and becomes enjoyable company. Their pleasure in one another is real, rather than merely a vehicle for symptoms or moral debate.

1. **The question returns.** The programmer has already sought reassurance from several people, but the narrator need not witness or catalogue every request. “It was the volume, really,” the programmer says. His wife arrives beside him: “You've said.” Her line reveals repetition without explaining it clinically.

1. **The teacher's practical care.** His wife, referred to as “the teacher,” hands him water as she speaks. Affection and mild exhaustion coexist. She meets the priest here and sees the two men getting along; their later school encounter is a reunion she welcomes, not a discovery that they know each other.

1. **The relationship begins.** The priest makes moral existence briefly feel bearable and enjoys being useful; the programmer finds warmth, wit, and temporary relief. The scene establishes the appeal of their friendship and the first small recurrence of doubt, without announcing the full OCD cycle or resolving it.

## scene 3 decisions

1. **The hallway.** The programmer waits outside a first-year CSPE classroom with his wife's forgotten lunch. She is referred to as **“the teacher.”** Their familiarity establishes the marriage.

1. **Class ends.** The teacher and priest bring the pupils into the hallway and line them up quietly before taking them outside for break. She is pleased to see the two men getting along again after the wedding.

1. **The prison remark.** Seeing black and grey uniforms, enforced silence, and children in a line, the programmer compares the school to a prison. The pupils' ordinary restlessness makes his comparison funny and slightly excessive.

1. **Uniforms and wealth.** The priest explains that uniforms reduce visible differences in parental wealth. The programmer points out a pupil's iPhone. Another child says he has no phone; someone laughs, and the child looks down. The teacher moves the line along.

1. **The fundraiser.** The teacher is helping organise a cake sale supporting older students travelling to an African country to help build houses. The priest is visiting to support the appeal.

1. **The lesson continues outside.** The CSPE class has been discussing European cooperation and the country's overseas “good works.” A pupil returns to the subject as the adults accompany them outside.

1. **The children's questions.** Why don't African countries have their own union to organise the work? The teacher explains that they do. Another child asks whether they have builders—and whether those builders get paid for teaching the visiting students.

1. **Who benefits?** Once the pupils are occupied, the priest praises how the trip changes students' lives. The programmer asks whose benefit governs the project. The teacher counters that the houses remain useful even if the visitors enjoy themselves.

1. **Solidarity and present privilege.** The priest invokes the country's colonial past as a source of sympathy and understanding. The programmer points out that roughly five million people enjoy an annual audience with the American president, EU membership, and profitable connections to multinational business. “We get into the White House every March carrying a bowl of shamrock.” Past oppression does not establish present powerlessness.

1. **His own participation.** The priest observes that the programmer's company benefits from those arrangements. “Yes. That's part of what I'm saying.” The programmer questions whether charity answers structural injustice while acknowledging his own advantages.

1. **Comic tenderness.** The discussion continues around fundraising tasks. The programmer helps; a pupil sells him a collapsed bun at a discount. The teacher has work to finish beyond adjudicating their argument.

1. **Housing at home.** Another teacher mentions moving back in with her parents because local housing is unaffordable. A child asks whether the older pupils could build her a house: people already know how to build houses. Housing abroad has been presented as a practical charitable task; at home, the adults suddenly stress its complexity.

1. **The young country.** The priest defends the country as still young. The departing teacher jokes about her own age; the programmer asks how long she is expected to wait.

1. **The embarrassment returns.** The programmer regrets exposing the phoneless child. The teacher offers a proportionate correction. He briefly accepts it, then revisits the precise words, laughter, and lowered eyes, seeking certainty about the harm.

1. **The priest's retreat.** Practical assistance restores his sense that something useful has been accomplished, allowing the larger objections to recede. The departing teacher's housing difficulty prepares the next scene's revelation: the programmer's home has gained more value in a year than his wife earns.

## overall story decisions

1. The story is narrated in first person by an unnamed priest whose account is intelligent, charming, and subtly unreliable.

1. The tone is comic fatalism with genuine tenderness and an Irish sarcastic twist. Humour comes through understatement, mock-formal language, affectionate insult, lingering Catholic vocabulary, bureaucratic absurdity, and serious arguments interrupted by food, drink, weather, repairs, or practical obligations. Dialogue is compressed and confrontational: petty incidents expand into moral disputes, comedy can drop abruptly into pain, and characters wound one another while remaining genuinely fond of each other. The prose avoids phonetic dialect and stage-Irish charm. Nobody announces love; characters perform it through attention, food, remembered preferences, and imperfect practical care.

1. He calls the other central character **“the programmer,”** ostensibly to protect his privacy. The label identifies the man by his respectable employment rather than his intellectual passion.

1. The country is never named but strongly resembles Ireland through colonial history, Catholic inheritance, independence mythology, emigration, rapid prosperity, multinational technology companies, housing pressure, and declining church attendance.

1. The programmer is a lapsed Catholic who feels little national attachment. He considers birthplace morally arbitrary rather than a source of pride.

1. He majored in computer science because programming had become the country's approved form of intelligence: modern, respectable, and well paid.

1. He chose anthropology as a minor almost randomly because he did not know what else to study. It unexpectedly became the part of his education he cared about most.

1. Anthropology exposed him to colonialism, social reproduction, structural inequality, cultural contingency, and institutional power.

1. His intellectual influences include Noam Chomsky, David Graeber, and Daniel Guérin. He combines their ideas imperfectly rather than possessing one coherent political philosophy.

1. He runs a technically sophisticated startup that is simultaneously semi-successful and semi-failing.

1. His education, contacts, and financial security gave him opportunities unavailable to equally talented people, making it difficult for him to interpret any success as deserved.

1. He became a programmer and entrepreneur partly to satisfy his father's ideal of ambition, usefulness, and respectable success.

1. He hates entrepreneurship and would prefer to pursue research, but has not admitted this fully to himself. He remains because of employees, investors, sunk costs, paternal expectations, and fear of failure.

1. His career participates in a national story in which software, startups, and foreign investment prove that the country has escaped its insecure past.

1. His parents loved him but had a chronically unhappy relationship marked by arguments, withdrawal, reconciliation, and grievances that never properly ended.

1. His father displays narcissistic behaviour: denying previous statements, reframing disagreement as ingratitude, claiming the son's achievements, and assigning failures entirely to him.

1. His father's gaslighting contributed to the programmer's lack of trust in his own memory, motives, and interpretations.

1. Alcoholism runs through his father's side of the family. The programmer drinks heavily, alternating between minimising the pattern and treating it as proof of inherited corruption.

1. He displayed mild obsessive-compulsive tendencies in childhood. Family instability, university independence, anthropology, alcohol, and adult responsibility contributed to a gradual escalation rather than one dramatic event causing his illness.

1. Computer science taught him to search for systems, root causes, hidden dependencies, and correctable errors. He consequently treats his moral life as malfunctioning code containing a discoverable bug.

1. Anthropology taught him that norms concerning property, class, work, gender, religion, and nationality change across cultures and history.

1. He distrusts authority, received wisdom, and apparently self-evident premises. This makes him perceptive but unable to accept provisional conclusions or unresolved uncertainty.

1. He sometimes commits the genetic fallacy in reverse: because a norm is historically contingent, he assumes that it must be unjust or useless.

1. His belief that he is awful functions as an unfalsifiable theory. Success proves privilege; failure proves irresponsibility; generosity proves manipulation; remorse proves narcissism; and forgiveness proves that other people cannot judge him accurately.

1. He has moral-scrupulosity OCD dominated by mental compulsions: rumination, memory review, motive-checking, research, rehearsed arguments, confession, and reassurance-seeking.

1. One physical clue appears only once: the priest notices that the skin across the programmer's knuckles is red and finely split, then assumes it is caused by cold weather.

1. The programmer makes real but ordinary mistakes while drinking: insensitive remarks, social misreadings, excessive disclosures, and tedious behaviour.

1. His friends genuinely like him. They correct him, accept proportionate apologies, and continue inviting him into their lives. He interprets their affection as something else he has not earned.

1. In his early twenties, he paid tax to the wrong jurisdiction. An accountant later advised that the amount was too small to justify correction because the administrative cost would exceed the disputed sum. He nevertheless calls himself a despicable tax cheat.

1. He once received a government-funded trip to Brazil to help organise a grant. The trip was authorised, the expenses were honest, and he did the required work, but the project accomplished little and he greatly enjoyed himself. He therefore fears eventual exposure as someone who stole a holiday from the public.

1. The fourth scene's concrete political conflict concerns the home he shares with his wife. They buy a modest house in a historically working-class neighbourhood that is rapidly becoming expensive. Soon afterwards, an estate agent reports that its value has risen in one year by more than his wife earns from teaching, a long-term neighbour says her adult children cannot afford to live nearby, and a new café advertises the area as an “undiscovered quarter.”

1. The programmer calls himself a gentrifier. His wife answers that they needed a home, bought a suitable house without evicting anyone, and cannot personally control the property market. He asks the priest whether someone can participate innocently in a process that predictably displaces other people. The discussion expands into multinational salaries, local wages, scarcity, unearned property wealth, class displacement, and aggregate harm. His OCD then eliminates every exit: remaining means benefiting, selling captures the profit, and selling below market feels paternalistic without changing surrounding prices.

1. The wife comes from another country and was raised in a stable, loving household. She is not organised around trauma and enjoys inhabiting immediate life.

1. She works as a teacher and is affectionate, empathetic, and attentive. She also has ordinary flaws and limits rather than functioning as a morally perfect caretaker.

1. Their marriage is loving but deteriorating. The story does not disclose whether it ultimately survives.

1. She wants the programmer to love himself and repeatedly reassures him because reassurance produces visible short-term relief. Neither recognises that they are participating in a compulsive loop.

1. She supports his friendship with the priest because she hopes another compassionate adult can reach him and share a burden she does not know how to carry.

1. The programmer places his wife on a pedestal. Ordinary irritation, boredom, criticism, or unwanted thoughts about her feel like acts of desecration for which he mentally punishes himself.

1. He sometimes says insensitive or cutting things to her and then accuses himself of abuse. When she rejects that description, he suggests that she may have normalised his behaviour, thereby discounting her authority over her own experience.

1. His wife cannot understand why love, forgiveness, and evidence never settle his doubts. She offers love as lived experience; he examines it as evidence.

1. The priest is intelligent, widely read, funny, and pastorally skilled. His warmth makes his eventual failure seductive and frustrating rather than merely reactionary.

1. He relies upon inherited norms, conventional moral formulas, and received wisdom. He assumes that ideas surviving across generations probably answer genuine human needs.

1. He reasons through anecdotes and personal experience rather than systems. A successful poor student, charitable employer, or admirable entrepreneur outweighs structural evidence in his imagination.

1. He treats existing institutions as ordinary reality while demanding extensive justification from anyone proposing change. The status quo receives a presumption of legitimacy.

1. He is deeply patriotic because his country genuinely fought against oppression. School history taught him a moral drama of occupation, sacrifice, resistance, and freedom.

1. He converts that true emancipatory history into continuing moral credit for present institutions. The programmer argues that ancestral suffering cannot permanently legitimise current power.

1. The priest experiences the programmer's lack of patriotism as bloodless ingratitude. The programmer experiences the priest's nationalism as survivorship bias transformed into identity.

1. The priest's congregation is shrinking as organised religion declines. Being needed by an unbeliever reassures him that priestly moral authority still matters.

1. Before speaking, the men vaguely know of each other. The priest has seen local coverage of the programmer's startup and heard the programmer's proud father describe it. The programmer has encountered the priest in a newspaper or radio discussion defending a national commemoration.

1. Their first meeting occurs at a wedding reception. The programmer is a friend of the groom; the priest is an old family friend who officiated and stayed for the reception. They meet at the coffee table.

1. They first connect through the priest's light complaint about the filter coffee: “I see they've kept something back for the penitential rite.” The joke establishes their comic rhythm before the programmer asks for reassurance.

1. The tipsy programmer has interrupted the groom's speech by prompting him too loudly to thank his brother Fergal. He fears that he disrupted the occasion and seeks reassurance from several people. The priest's proportionate reassurance produces immediate relief that soon fades. The teacher arrives with water and gently exposes the repetition: “You've said.”

1. Their second meeting also happens by coincidence. The priest visits the secondary school where the teacher teaches first-year CSPE, supporting a fundraiser for older students' overseas building trip. The programmer arrives to deliver her forgotten lunch, and she is pleased to see the men getting along again after the wedding.

1. The school meeting becomes the first explicit argument about institutional power. After watching the priest field children's questions, the programmer objects to an unelected celibate institution retaining influence over schools and children's moral formation. The priest answers through memories of schools providing safety, books, meals, and adults who noticed neglected children.

1. The programmer remembers school as classification, public correction, compulsory performance, and punishment for failing to inhabit the approved kind of child. His wife refuses either total account: a school can be protective, oppressive, and ordinary at the same time.

1. As the school meeting ends, another teacher mentions leaving because local rent has become unaffordable. Soon afterwards the programmer receives the valuation showing that his house has gained more in a year than his wife earns from teaching. This carries the argument about institutional power into the fourth scene's property conflict.

1. The programmer returns with increasingly refined questions. Their conversations become a compulsive substitute for confession, with each answer generating another objection.

1. The programmer likes the priest because he is warm, funny, and capable of making moral existence briefly feel bearable.

1. The priest genuinely enjoys the programmer's wit, education, and intellectual ferocity. He also enjoys feeling necessary, authoritative, and relevant.

1. The priest and the programmer genuinely find one another funny. The priest enjoys the programmer's precision, irreverence, and escalating hypotheticals; the programmer enjoys the priest's clerical deadpan and comic timing. Their recurring jokes make the meetings a real friendship rather than a sequence of consultations. Humour creates intimacy but also sometimes lets the priest deflect an argument just as it becomes dangerous. Near the ending, the absence of an expected joke can show that something between them has hardened.

1. Their debates range across merit, work, property, inequality, class conflict, billionaires, nationalism, charity, institutional power, personal responsibility, and changing social norms. The billionaire question centres on whether philanthropy can justify unaccountable private power; the priest reaches for beneficent individuals, while the programmer asks when accumulated advantage becomes illegitimate and then turns the accusation against himself.

1. Their intellectual opposition is genuine: the programmer sees systems so readily that persons and proportion can disappear; the priest sees persons and stories so readily that systems disappear.

1. The reader initially encounters an intelligent man raising reasonable moral questions. Repetition, shrinking relief, lost sleep, drinking, impaired attention, and repeated reassurance gradually reveal serious OCD.

1. The programmer's illness does not invalidate his political analysis. The social order really is unjust, although his attempt to calculate his exact personal culpability is compulsive and impossible.

1. The programmer dismantles many of the priest's arguments but receives neither absolution nor redemption. He changes nothing outwardly and leaves carrying an injustice he cannot resolve.

1. The priest understands many individual objections but never integrates them into a systemic critique. His mind repeatedly settles back into the assumptions of the status quo.

1. The priest briefly encounters the same moral burden but successfully puts it aside. His ability to continue comfortably makes him increasingly hateable.

1. The reader should finish wanting the priest to truly listen and wanting the programmer to extend himself the humanity he readily gives other people.

1. Political and systemic injustice forms the story's foreground. The characters' family histories, marriage, alcoholism, careers, and mental health remain scaffolding that explains the pressure behind their arguments.

1. The full biographies exist in the author's notes, but the story reveals them only through compressed allusions: split knuckles, a message from the wife, an inherited phrase, an empty pew, an accepted drink, or a briefly mentioned memory.

1. The story is organised around repeated conversations that begin with concrete cases and widen into political arguments about merit, public money, nationalism, charity, redistribution, institutional authority, and ethical life within unjust systems.

1. Every intellectual discussion must also change the relationship: reassurance is sought, authority is asserted, affection deepens, dependence grows, or patience erodes. The characters never become neutral mouthpieces for essays.

1. The story uses autobiographical emotional material as raw material while substantially altering education, career, family, marriage, chronology, locations, and events. It must not function as therapy, confession, or a request for the reader to acquit the author.

1. Every autobiographically inspired scene must stand without knowledge of the author's life, materially transform its source, and give the other characters independent agency.

1. The priest, programmer, wife, and country remain unnamed, while minor characters may be named when specificity helps the scene. Real authors, theories, and historical ideas are named. This gives the story parable-like distance without making its intellectual world abstract.
